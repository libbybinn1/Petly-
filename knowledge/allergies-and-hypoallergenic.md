# Allergies and the Hypoallergenic Claim

## No dog or cat is hypoallergenic
The allergens are proteins, not hair. In cats the main one is Fel d 1,
produced in saliva and sebaceous glands and spread over the coat by grooming;
in dogs the equivalents are Can f 1 and related proteins found in saliva,
skin and urine. Every dog and every cat produces them. Hair only matters as
the vehicle that carries dander into the air and onto furniture.

## Low-shedding breeds reduce spread, not production
Poodles, poodle crosses, bichons, schnauzers and Portuguese water dogs shed
far less coat, so less allergen is distributed around the home and grooming
is easier to control. This helps some people. What it does not do is make the
dog allergen-free, and studies comparing allergen levels in homes with
so-called hypoallergenic breeds against other breeds have not found a
consistent difference. An adopter should be told this plainly before a
placement is made on the strength of a breed label.

Sphynx cats are frequently misdescribed as hypoallergenic. They have no
protective coat, so skin oils and Fel d 1 accumulate directly on the skin and
they need regular bathing; they are not a safe choice for an allergic
household. Claims made for Siberian and Balinese cats are not well supported
either.

## Small mammals are often the stronger allergen
Rats, mice, guinea pigs and rabbits are among the best-documented causes of
animal allergy, largely from urinary proteins carried on bedding dust. In
laboratory settings this is a recognised occupational disease. A household
that copes with a cat may react badly to a pair of rats, and the small size of
the animal is no protection at all.

## Birds cause a different problem
Feather dust and dried droppings can cause hypersensitivity pneumonitis -
bird fancier's lung - which is a lung disease rather than an allergy and can
become permanent. Cockatoos, cockatiels and doves are the dustiest species.
Any household with an existing respiratory condition should be advised
carefully before a bird is placed, and the room needs real ventilation.

## Reptiles, amphibians and fish
These produce essentially no dander and are a genuine option for a household
with severe mammalian allergy. The trade-off is hygiene rather than allergy:
reptiles and amphibians commonly carry salmonella, so hand-washing
discipline is required and they are not recommended where there are children
under five.

## What actually reduces exposure
Keep the animal out of the bedroom entirely. Use HEPA filtration in the rooms
the animal uses. Wash bedding weekly at high temperature. Have a non-allergic
member of the household do the grooming and the cage cleaning, outdoors where
possible. Hard floors hold far less allergen than carpet. Treat the person as
well as the environment: allergic rhinitis and asthma respond to proper
medical management, and an adopter who has never seen a doctor about their
symptoms has options they do not know about.

## Test before adopting, not after
The reliable predictor is direct exposure to the individual animal, because
allergen production varies between animals of the same breed. Arrange
repeated visits and time in the same room before committing. Where a
household member has asthma triggered by that species, a placement made on
the promise that it will be managed is a placement likely to be reversed, and
a returned animal is a worse outcome than a delayed adoption.
