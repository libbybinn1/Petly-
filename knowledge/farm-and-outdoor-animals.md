# Farm and Outdoor Animals

## Check the law before assessing the home
Goats, pigs, poultry and equines are usually classified as livestock rather
than pets, and local planning rules, tenancy agreements and municipal bylaws
frequently prohibit them on residential land regardless of how large the
garden is. Roosters are banned in many built-up areas on noise grounds. A
placement that ignores this ends with the animal removed, so zoning is a hard
constraint and should be confirmed in writing before an adopter is assessed
on anything else.

## None of these animals can live alone
Goats, chickens, ducks, alpacas, horses and donkeys are all herd or flock
species. A single one kept as a companion animal is chronically stressed,
vocal and prone to escape. Any placement of one of these animals must either
be a pair or into an existing group of the same species. This is not a
preference that can be traded off against a better score elsewhere.

## Goats
Lifespan twelve to eighteen years. They browse rather than graze, so they
strip shrubs, trees and washing lines and are poor lawnmowers. Fencing has to
assume a climber and a chewer; stock fencing with no footholds and no
adjacent structures. Hooves need trimming every six to ten weeks and they
need a dry, draught-free shelter, because goats tolerate cold far better than
rain. Castrated males on a grain-heavy diet are prone to urinary stones,
which are an emergency, so their diet must be hay-based.

## Miniature horses
Lifespan twenty-five to thirty-five years, which is a longer commitment than
almost any other adoptable animal. The common mistake is treating small size
as low requirement: they are extremely prone to obesity and laminitis on rich
pasture and usually need a restricted dry lot or a grazing muzzle rather than
free grass. They need a farrier every six to eight weeks, annual dentistry
and vaccination, and the attention of an equine vet. They are herd animals
and need equine company.

## Pot-bellied and other pet pigs
Lifespan twelve to twenty years, and adults reach fifty to a hundred and
twenty kilograms. "Micro", "teacup" and "mini" pigs are a marketing claim,
not a size; most are sold as piglets and surrendered at two years old when
their true size becomes clear, and this is the single commonest reason pigs
enter rescue. They root by instinct and will turn a lawn over completely.
They cannot sweat, so they need shade and a wallow or mud area in summer.
They are highly intelligent, food-obsessed and easily trained, and they need
a strictly measured diet.

## Chickens
Flock birds; keep three or more. Guidance converges on roughly a third of a
square metre of coop floor per bird and at least a square metre of run,
with more being better. The coop must be genuinely predator-proof, including
against digging, and a run roof deters birds of prey. Laying declines sharply
after two or three years while the birds live six to ten, so an adopter must
want the animals rather than the eggs. Red mite and internal parasites need
routine attention.

## Ducks
Ducks need water deep enough to submerge their heads and preferably to swim
in; without clean bathing water they cannot maintain waterproof plumage.
They are dramatically messier than chickens, turning any small water source
into mud within days. Female ducks quack loudly and often, which makes them a
worse neighbour than hens. They should never be kept singly.

## Alpacas
Lifespan fifteen to twenty years. Herd animals, with two as an absolute
minimum and three preferred. They must be shorn annually or they suffer heat
stress, and their toenails need periodic trimming. They need pasture,
shelter, and protection from loose dogs, which are their main cause of
serious injury. They are gentle but generally do not want to be touched.

## Miniature donkeys
Lifespan twenty-five to thirty-five years. Donkeys evolved in arid country
and become obese and laminitic on good grass, so they need a dry lot, barley
straw as the bulk of the diet, and only limited grazing. Their coats are not
waterproof, so unlike horses they need genuine shelter from rain. They form
extremely strong pair bonds and separating a bonded pair causes serious
distress. Their bray carries for a considerable distance, which matters for
neighbours.
