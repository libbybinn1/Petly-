# Apartment Living

## Floor area is rarely the limiting factor
Flats fail animals for reasons that have little to do with square metres:
there is nowhere to toilet, nowhere to decompress, neighbours on the other
side of the wall, four flights of stairs between the animal and the street,
and a balcony that is a hazard rather than a garden. A large flat with none of
those problems solved is worse for a dog than a small house with a yard.

## Animals that genuinely suit a flat
Retired racing greyhounds and whippets are the classic surprise: they sprint
briefly and then sleep for most of the day, and they are quiet. Companion
breeds bred for company rather than work - cavaliers, bichons, Maltese, shih
tzus - adapt well provided they are not left alone all day. Older dogs of
almost any breed are easier in a flat than young ones. Cats do well indoors
given vertical space, scratching posts and a window to watch. Rabbits kept in
a secure indoor pen or free-roaming are well suited to flats, as are guinea
pigs, small rodents, most reptiles, and quiet birds such as doves, canaries
and budgerigars.

## Animals that do not
Huskies and malamutes are vocal, heat-intolerant and expert escapers. Herding
breeds need a job and invent one indoors. Large working and guarding breeds
need space and purpose. Among birds, cockatoos, macaws and sun conures are
loud enough to be heard clearly through a party wall and will generate
complaints within weeks. Roosters, ducks, goats and pigs do not belong in a
flat under any arrangement.

## Noise is the commonest reason a flat placement fails
Separation-related barking is the single largest source of neighbour
complaints and one of the most frequent reasons a dog is returned. A dog left
alone for nine hours in a flat is both a welfare problem and a neighbour
problem, and no amount of enrichment fully substitutes for company. Ask about
working hours before assessing anything else.

## Stairs and lifts
A brachycephalic dog cannot be walked far in summer heat and should not be
carried up several flights daily either. Long-backed breeds such as
dachshunds should not use stairs repeatedly, and senior or arthritic animals
cannot. If the animal will need carrying, the adopter must be able to lift its
adult weight every day for years, not once at the home visit.

## Balconies
Balconies are not gardens. Cats fall from them - high-rise syndrome is a
recognised presentation in urban veterinary practice - and a balcony used as
cat access needs netting or a solid barrier. Rabbits, guinea pigs and
reptiles must never be left on a balcony in direct sun, where temperatures
rise fast enough to kill. A balcony does not count as outdoor space for
matching purposes.

## A garden is a decompression space, not exercise
A secure garden raises a home's effective capacity by roughly one size band,
because it solves toileting, sniffing and a place to be outside. It does not
replace walks: a dog left in a garden all day is a dog alone outdoors.

## Permission
Building bylaws, tenancy agreements and body-corporate rules often limit the
number, size or species of animals kept. Confirm this before the adoption
rather than after it, because a placement reversed by a landlord is a
returned animal.
