# Senior Animals and Animals with Special Needs

## What counts as senior
Roughly seven years and above for large dogs, ten for small dogs and cats,
four for rabbits and guinea pigs, and two for hamsters. Senior animals
typically need veterinary monitoring, joint support, softer bedding, easier
access to litter trays and a calmer environment.

## Senior animals are often excellent matches
Their temperament is established rather than speculative, they are usually
house-trained, and their exercise needs are modest. For a calm adult household
with limited time, a senior animal is frequently a better match than a young
one. This should raise a match score, not lower it.

## Special care requires experience
Animals with ongoing medical needs - prescription diets, daily medication,
mobility limitations, chronic conditions - need an adopter who can sustain the
routine and absorb the cost. A first-time owner is rarely a safe placement,
not from lack of goodwill but from lack of accumulated judgement about when
something is wrong.

## Behavioural special needs
Anxious animals need predictable routines, quiet homes and an adopter who
accepts a long settling period. Progress is measured in months. Placing an
anxious animal into a busy household with young children usually fails.

## Brachycephalic breeds
Flat-faced breeds such as bulldogs and pugs cannot regulate temperature or
tolerate strenuous exercise. They need short, cool walks and should never be
matched to an adopter seeking a running companion.
