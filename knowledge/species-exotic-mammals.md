# Exotic Mammals: Ferrets, Chinchillas, Hedgehogs, Rats and Relatives

## Small does not mean easy
These species are usually adopted on the assumption that a small animal is a
small commitment. In practice most of them are harder to keep well than a
cat: they have narrower temperature tolerances, more specific diets, stronger
social needs, and far fewer vets willing to treat them. Confirm that an
exotics vet is reachable before placing any animal in this group, because the
nearest one may be an hour away and charge accordingly.

## Ferrets
Lifespan six to ten years. Obligate carnivores, so a diet of high-protein
meat-based food, not rodent or rabbit mix. Highly social and best kept in
pairs or small groups. They sleep eighteen hours a day and are relentlessly
busy for the other six, needing several hours outside the cage in a
ferret-proofed room; they squeeze through any gap wider than their skull and
swallow rubber and foam, which causes fatal blockages. They need vaccinating
against canine distemper, which is lethal to them. Adrenal disease and
insulinoma are common in middle age. They have a distinct musky smell that
washing does not remove and descenting does not fix.

## Chinchillas
Lifespan fifteen to twenty years, which makes them a longer commitment than
most dogs. They come from the high Andes and cannot shed heat: above roughly
25C they are at real risk of heat stroke, so an air-conditioned room is a
requirement in a hot climate, not a comfort. Their fur is too dense to be
washed with water and mats if it gets wet; they bathe in volcanic dust two or
three times a week instead. The diet is grass hay and a plain pellet, with no
fruit, nuts or seeds. They are social, best kept in pairs, and most of them
dislike being held however tame they become - they are animals to watch and
sit with rather than cuddle.

## African pygmy hedgehogs
Lifespan four to six years. Solitary by nature and must be housed alone.
Strictly nocturnal, and noisy about it: a wheel running all night rules out a
bedroom. They need an ambient temperature of 24-27C; below that they attempt
hibernation, which they are not physiologically equipped to survive in
captivity and which is frequently fatal. The diet is insect-based with a
high-protein low-fat dry food, and obesity is the commonest health problem
after that. They huff and ball up when startled, so they suit a patient
adopter rather than a child wanting a responsive pet. They are prohibited in
some jurisdictions.

## Fancy rats
Lifespan two to three years, and that short span is the hardest part of
keeping them, because they are affectionate and genuinely trainable. They are
intensely social and must never be kept alone; a single rat kept with a
devoted owner is still a lonely rat. They need a tall multi-level cage,
daily handling, and an exotics vet, because chronic mycoplasma respiratory
disease affects most colonies and mammary tumours are common in unspayed
females. They are among the best small pets for an older child who wants an
animal that interacts.

## Fancy mice and gerbils
Mice live eighteen months to two and a half years. Females live well in small
groups; adult males usually fight and have a strong odour. Gerbils live three
to four years, should be kept in same-sex pairs, and need at least twenty
centimetres of substrate to build the burrow systems that occupy most of
their waking life - a shallow cage frustrates the behaviour they most need to
perform. Gerbils come from dry grassland and do badly in humid conditions.
Neither species enjoys being picked up.

## Degus
Lifespan six to eight years. Unusually for a rodent they are diurnal, so they
are awake when their household is. They are colony animals and should be kept
in groups. They cannot metabolise sugar: fruit, honey and sweet treats cause
diabetes and cataracts, which is why their diet must be strictly controlled
hay, guinea-pig pellets and dried herbs. They need constant access to wood to
gnaw and a dust bath.

## Sugar gliders
Lifespan ten to fifteen years. Nocturnal marsupials that live in colonies and
should never be kept alone; a solitary glider self-mutilates. They need a
tall aviary-style cage with room to glide, and a specialised diet of nectar
supplement, insects and fruit rather than a packaged "glider food". They are
loud at night, they scent-mark, and they are restricted or prohibited in a
number of jurisdictions. They are among the most frequently surrendered
exotic pets.
