# Reptiles and Amphibians

## The equipment is the commitment
A reptile does not regulate its own body temperature, so the enclosure does
it instead. Almost every health problem seen in captive reptiles traces back
to equipment: no thermostat, no ultraviolet light, an enclosure too small to
hold a temperature gradient, or the wrong humidity. The animal is usually the
cheapest part of the placement, and an adopter who cannot fund the setup
cannot keep the animal well however willing they are.

Every heat source must run through a thermostat. An unregulated heat mat or
bulb causes burns and overheating, and both are common.

## Bearded dragons
Lifespan eight to twelve years. An adult needs an enclosure of roughly 120 by
60 by 60 centimetres with a basking surface at 38-42C and a cool end at
22-26C, plus a UVB tube replaced every six to twelve months even while it
still emits visible light. They are omnivores: leafy greens and vegetables
with appropriately sized insects dusted in calcium. Insufficient UVB or
calcium causes metabolic bone disease, which is disfiguring and irreversible.
Well-kept dragons are genuinely tame and tolerate handling well.

## Leopard geckos
Lifespan fifteen to twenty years, occasionally longer. Crepuscular and
ground-dwelling, so floor area matters more than height; roughly 90 by 45
centimetres for one adult. Belly heat from a thermostatted mat with a warm
end of about 32C, a low level of UVB, and several hides including a humid one
for shedding. Insectivorous. They are among the calmest reptiles to handle
and a reasonable first reptile.

## Corn snakes and ball pythons
Corn snakes live fifteen to twenty years and are the most forgiving snake to
keep: a 120-centimetre vivarium, a warm end around 28-30C, and a
frozen-thawed rodent every week or two. They are determined escapers and a
poorly latched door is the usual way one is lost.

Ball pythons live twenty to thirty years, need a warm spot of about 31C and
humidity in the region of 55-65 per cent, and routinely refuse food for weeks
or months in winter. That fast is normal behaviour in a healthy animal at a
stable weight, and an adopter who panics and force-feeds does harm.

## Tortoises
Mediterranean species such as Greek, Hermann's and Russian tortoises live
fifty to a hundred years and frequently outlive their owners, so a placement
should name a successor. They need a large outdoor grazing area in warm
months, a dry shelter, and a diet of weeds and leafy plants that is high in
fibre and low in protein. Fruit, pet-shop pellets and any animal protein
cause shell pyramiding and kidney damage. Most species brumate in winter and
need a safe, cool, monitored place to do it.

## Aquatic turtles
Red-eared sliders reach twenty to thirty centimetres and commonly live twenty
to thirty years, sometimes longer. An adult needs several hundred litres of
water with strong
filtration, a dry basking platform under both heat and UVB, and regular water
changes. They are the most commonly surrendered reptile precisely because the
adult requirements bear no relation to the hatchling sold in a bowl.

## Axolotls
Lifespan ten to fifteen years. Fully aquatic, and the one animal here that
needs to be kept cool: 16-18C, which in a warm climate means a chiller rather
than a heater. They must not be housed with fish, which nip their gills, and
they should not be handled, as their skin has no protective scales. They are
sensitive to ammonia, so the tank must be cycled before the animal arrives.

## Hygiene and households with young children
Reptiles and amphibians commonly carry salmonella in their gut without being
ill. Public health guidance is consistent: they are not recommended for
households with children under five, with anyone pregnant, or with anyone
immunocompromised. Where they are kept, hand-washing after every contact is
not optional, and the animal should never be handled in a kitchen.
