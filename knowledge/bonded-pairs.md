# Bonded Pairs and Groups

## What a bonded pair is
Two animals that have formed a specific attachment to each other, usually
shown by sleeping in contact, grooming each other and calling when separated.
It is not the same as two animals that merely tolerate sharing a hutch. A
recorded bond is a clinical finding about those two animals, not a
convenience for the shelter, and it should be treated as part of the
animals' care requirements.

## Species where bonds are the normal state
Rabbits, guinea pigs, rats, mice, gerbils, degus, chinchillas, ferrets and
sugar gliders are social by default and should be placed in pairs or groups.
So are budgerigars, lovebirds, finches, canaries kept in aviaries, doves,
chickens, ducks, goats, alpacas and donkeys. For these species the question
is not whether a pair is acceptable but whether a single animal is.

Cats and dogs are different: most are content alone with human company, but
individual bonded pairs do occur, usually littermates or animals that have
lived together for years. These bonds are real and worth honouring where
they exist.

## What separation actually does
In rabbits the risk is physical. A rabbit that loses its partner may stop
eating, and a rabbit that stops eating develops gut stasis, which is
frequently fatal without prompt veterinary treatment. Guinea pigs become
withdrawn and lose weight. Bonded ferrets and rats become inactive and
lose condition. Parrots and lovebirds separated from a bonded mate commonly
begin feather-plucking.

Rebonding a rabbit to a new partner is possible but takes weeks of
supervised introduction on neutral ground and sometimes fails outright. The
existing bond is always the safer option.

## The practical cost of a pair
Food, bedding and veterinary costs roughly double. Space requirements rise
but less than double, since the animals share it and benefit from company.
Daily care time barely changes. A pair is therefore a smaller increase in
effort than most adopters expect, and it is worth saying so plainly when an
adopter hesitates.

## How this should affect a match
An adopter who can take two animals of the same species is the better
placement for a bonded pair even if a single-animal household scored
marginally higher on other criteria. The correct response to a bonded pair
with no suitable adopter is to widen the search, not to split the pair to
make an existing candidate fit. Splitting should be considered only on
veterinary or behavioural advice about those specific animals.

## The exceptions that must be housed alone
Syrian hamsters are solitary and will fight, sometimes fatally, if housed
together. African pygmy hedgehogs are solitary. Adult male mice usually
fight. Most reptiles are solitary and some will injure or eat a cage-mate.
For these species, group housing is the welfare failure and single housing is
correct.

## Littermate dogs are a separate question
Two puppies raised together sometimes become so focused on each other that
they fail to bond with people, struggle to be separated even briefly, and
learn poorly. This is a training and development problem rather than a bond
to protect, and behaviourists often recommend deliberate separate handling or
separate homes. Do not confuse it with an adult bonded pair.
